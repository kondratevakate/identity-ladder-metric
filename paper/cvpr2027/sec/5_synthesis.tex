\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:synthesis}
The conditional mean of the hidden content given the context minimizes expected squared error, and a fill drawn from the true conditional distribution has twice that error, because it adds an independent draw of the unpredictable part; this is the distortion side of the perception-distortion tradeoff~\cite{blau2018}.
Put simply, when the hidden content cannot be predicted, the safest guess under squared error is the average, and a realistic person scores worse.
The softened same person and the mean of the 16 nearest people are empirical stand-ins for that mean.
The fact itself is elementary.
What the ladder adds is its size on real anatomy: no error or similarity metric places the person's own second image ahead of its softened version in any image set (Table~\ref{tab:tests}), and set-level metrics trade this reward for the opposite blind spot.
A pixel score alone cannot show whether a fill kept the person.
We therefore suggest reporting three numbers next to any inpainting score on the same masks: the dataset average score, the score for the mean of the 16 nearest people chosen from the context, and the score for a random person.
Under \ac{MSE} alone, beating the nearest-people mean can also mean averaging more, which is why the softened control belongs next to it.
A conditional mean remains the right answer when the hidden content is truly ambiguous; the controls make that choice visible.

\paragraph{A recipe for inpainting benchmarks.}
For each hidden region, a benchmark can compute the region's dataset average over unrelated people, the mean of the 16 people whose context rings are closest, and 16 random people, all using the same valid pixels as the submitted infills.
Scores are then reported on the random-person scale of Eq.~\ref{eq:anchors}, so that a submission at 0.5 is as close as the dataset average, and its gap to a context search is read against that set's own nearest-people score (0.20 to 0.40 at 1/8).
Where a dataset has second images of the same people, the softened second image adds a direct test of whether the metric rewards averaging, and a set-level metric on the same fills shows whether the submission has lost realism.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The ladder calibrates metrics and does not by itself rank models.
The pretrained models run mostly outside their training domain (a scene model on gray aligned faces, a tumor-inpainting model on healthy brains and on \mfBratsQtimN{} of 221 people).
Alignment is the main confounder outside faces: misalignment adds to every distance in brain, retina and iris, most in mirrored comparisons with the opposite side.
It does not explain the averaging reward alone, because faces, whose alignment passed visual \ac{QC}, fail T2 in the same way (Table~\ref{tab:tests}).
The cross-dataset summaries and tests were defined after the per-dataset results and are exploratory; only 23 QTIM people have a second scan, two of nine image sets record families, and the set-level exclusion rule was fixed after seeing one value.
\ac{LPIPS}, topology and set-level metrics still use about 1/5 of the object in faces, retina and iris.
The ladder measures closeness to known references, not clinical or biometric correctness: a fill at the same person's position can still miss a lesion.

\paragraph{Ethics and data.}
We collected no new data and use nine public image sets under their terms: QTIM and GRAPE (CC0), IXI (CC BY-SA 3.0), PAPILA (GPL 3.0+), CelebA (non-commercial research only), IMDb-WIKI (academic research only), and UTIRIS and UPOL (free with citation).
QTIM, GRAPE and PAPILA report ethics approval and signed informed consent; CelebA and IMDb-WIKI are Internet images of public figures (Supplementary Section~S2).
Faces, irises and retinas are biometric, so we show one QTIM brain scan (CC0) and otherwise only population averages or maps.
We did not balance the image sets by sex, age or ancestry, so the distances describe these cohorts, not clinical populations.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
References of known relationship turn a single inpainting score into a position on a ladder from the same person to a random one.
On nine image sets, error and similarity metrics reward averaging, set-level metrics barely separate the right person from a random one, and pretrained models score close to a retrieval baseline that needs no model.
The three controls cost no model and no labels and show which of these failures a new model has.
