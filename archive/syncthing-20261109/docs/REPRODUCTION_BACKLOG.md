# Reproduction backlog: literature for the identity-ladder metric

Purpose: independent literature review (no paper draft read) and a list of experiments worth reproducing or borrowing for the claim that pointwise metrics and pointwise-label downstream checks reward regression to the mean in BraTS inpainting. Date: 2026-11-01. Already verified elsewhere and not repeated here: Kofler 2023 (arXiv:2305.08992), Zhang, Weng, Chen 2025 (arXiv:2511.20202), Henschel 2020 FastSurfer, Wang 2004 SSIM, Maier-Hein 2024 Metrics Reloaded. Zhang and Skolnick 2004 (TM-score), Mariani et al. 2013 (lDDT) and Chen et al. 2010 (MolProbity) were also verified elsewhere, but they are entered in theme 7 (T7-01, T7-05, T7-06) because that theme needs an experiment for each; their metadata and abstracts were nevertheless re-checked against Crossref, OpenAlex and PubMed. Entries per theme: 10, 9, 7, 7, 12, 6 and 25 for themes 1 to 7 (76 in all).

Verification: every entry was resolved by DOI or arXiv ID against Crossref (title, authors, year, volume, pages, DOI) and cross-checked with a second source (OpenAlex, PubMed, arXiv, Consensus, or the publisher/proceedings page). The "What it shows" sentence is taken only from the retrieved abstract. Where an abstract was read only in part it is phrased narrowly. Journal abbreviations follow NLM style but were not checked against the NLM catalogue. LNCS volume numbers are omitted because no database confirmed them. Code links marked "repo exists" were confirmed through the GitHub API (existence, licence); whether a repo is the official one is stated only where the abstract or paper says so.

Priority key: high = directly tests or operationalises the claim and has code; medium = supplies a calibration number or a baseline; low = background or theory.

## Papers that challenge our claim

The claim is not contradicted outright by any retrieved abstract. These items limit or complicate it and should be addressed in the paper:

1. Top pointwise scores may not mean blur. Durrer et al. (T2-08, T2-09) report best SSIM/PSNR/MSE for diffusion models, and the fastWDM3D abstract states that earlier DDPM entries gave qualitatively convincing results; the audit must show empirically (mean-fill and blur arms) that leaderboard order really tracks blur, not assume it.
2. The downstream check may be uninformative rather than biased. SynthSeg (T3-02) and the motion-robustness study (T3-07) indicate that modern segmenters are robust to degraded input; this weakens "downstream Dice is a fair arbiter" but also means the bias may be small, so quantify it before claiming it. No paper was found that directly shows blurrier inputs segmenting closer to an oracle; this part of the claim is currently unsupported by literature and needs our own experiment.
3. Realism measures are not a clean fix. T6-02 (medical-trained extractors not better than ImageNet) and T6-03 (FID bias) show that FID-type measures have their own failure modes; T1-10 warns that realism-driven losses can hallucinate.
4. The ladder ordering is not uniform. T5-05 finds MZ<DZ differences only in some regions; T5-09 reports heritability of 0.2-0.5, so the DZ/sibling rung may sit close to strangers; T5-08 reports test-retest ICC of 0.65-0.85, so the same-person floor itself is noisy; T5-10 shows hemispheres are systematically asymmetric.
5. The stranger null is not one distribution (theme 7). Ali et al. (T7-19) find that likelihood ratios from the same face system vary significantly with the databases used to model the two hypotheses; the NIST demographic report (T7-21) states that false-positive rates often vary by factors of 10 to beyond 100 across groups; Yager and Dunstone (T7-16) argue systems should be assessed per user, not by collective statistics; the KING and PC-Relate abstracts (T7-22, T7-23) report that earlier relatedness estimators are biased or limited under population structure; Rabin et al. (T7-25) adapt the threshold per query. A single pooled stranger distribution would be miscalibrated for subgroups, so c(d) must be computed against matched strangers and checked for sample invariance.
6. A calibrated number is not a certificate of identity. Muchmore et al. (T7-10) state that a raw similarity value has no direct link to the chance that two molecules are equally active, which they address with a calibration framework; Xu and Zhang (T7-02) call TM-score = 0.5 an approximate criterion, with a phase transition rather than a sharp boundary; Lund and Iyer (T7-20) argue a likelihood ratio needs an uncertainty characterisation, so a point value of c(d) without an interval overstates precision.
7. Rungs need not be ordered or distinct. Daugman and Downing (T7-12) find that irises of monozygotic twins and the left and right eyes of one person follow the same distribution as unrelated eyes; Sun et al. (T7-13) find twin impostors are harder to separate for finger and face but not significantly for iris; Speed and Balding (T7-24) note that actual genome sharing can differ considerably from pedigree expectation. For some brain features the MZ, other-hemisphere and DZ rungs may therefore coincide with the stranger rung, which would be a finding about the feature, not an error of the ladder (see also item 4).
8. Model confidence may not be a stand-in for the ground-truth rung. The CASP16 assessment (T7-08) reports that model-selection performance varied significantly across target categories; the self-estimate route should be tested, not assumed.
